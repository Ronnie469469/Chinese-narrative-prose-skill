# Style Bible

This file is the fixed style system for `$narrative-prose`. It distills publicly described New Concept Writing principles and observable patterns from public book metadata, contents, commentary, and event reports. It is not a corpus and must not be used to reproduce source texts.

## Core Aesthetic

The target style is a contemporary Chinese narrative prose voice shaped by three commitments:

- **New thought:** refuse ready-made school-essay conclusions; approach ordinary material through a personal angle, a quiet contradiction, or a changed understanding.
- **New expression:** use an individual speaking rhythm; avoid slogans, formulaic lyricism, and the same moral everyone expects.
- **True experience:** make emotion pass through concrete life: rooms, weather, school corridors, train stations, meals, phone screens, family habits, city light, old objects, repeated gestures.

The prose should feel like a person remembering, noticing, and revising their understanding in real time. It may be youthful, but it should not be naive. It may be lyrical, but the lyricism must be earned by narrative pressure.

## Stable Features

- Start from a small aperture: one object, one errand, one sentence overheard, one departure, one unfinished conversation.
- Let the visible story carry an invisible question: what changed in me, what I failed to understand, what I can no longer return to, or what ordinary thing suddenly became difficult.
- Use scenes instead of declarations. A paragraph should often be anchored by a place, time, person, object, sound, smell, or bodily action.
- Prefer delayed emotion. The narrator notices first, interprets later, and only names the emotion after evidence has accumulated.
- Keep thought mobile. The essay can move between memory, present observation, imagined alternative, and social reality, but each jump needs an image, object, phrase, or action as a hinge.
- Make language personal. The voice may contain plain speech, sudden exactness, light irony, and quiet self-correction.
- End with resonance, not verdict. Return to a detail and let it hold more meaning than it held at the beginning.

## Six-Layer Style Matrix

### Theme

Preferred themes:

- Growth and belated understanding.
- Leaving home, returning home, and discovering that return is partial.
- Family intimacy mixed with misunderstanding, silence, duty, or tenderness.
- School, youth, friendship, first love, rivalry, embarrassment, and private rituals.
- City life, rented rooms, transport, crowds, anonymity, and loneliness.
- Ordinary people and social changes seen through a personal witness.

The theme must have two layers:

- Surface: what happened.
- Understory: what the narrator only understands after time, distance, loss, or repetition.

### Narrative

Common narrative shapes:

- **Memory triggered by present:** begin with a present object or scene, enter memory, return changed.
- **One day as container:** a simple day carries an old conflict or long relationship.
- **Person through fragments:** show a person through habits, sentences, and repeated gestures rather than biography.
- **Journey and threshold:** station, road, moving house, graduation, hospital, exam, or farewell becomes a border.
- **Social observation through self:** a public change is understood through one private consequence.

Avoid a plot that only reports events. Each event must shift the narrator's relation to the theme.

### Detail

Details should be ordinary, exact, and load-bearing:

- Objects: ticket, bowl, umbrella, key, desk, school uniform, old phone, exercise book, medicine box.
- Actions: folding, waiting, stirring, deleting, turning away, pretending not to hear, wiping, packing, checking time.
- Places: corridor, balcony, classroom, station, kitchen, clinic, convenience store, bus stop, rented room.
- Senses: dim light, detergent smell, fan noise, winter air, damp clothes, train broadcast, phone vibration.

Use an object thread across the essay. Introduce it early, alter its meaning midway, and recover it near the end.

### Emotion

Emotion should move through five stages:

1. **Pressure:** something small feels disproportionate.
2. **Deflection:** the narrator jokes, observes, or changes topic.
3. **Accumulation:** repeated details reveal what is actually at stake.
4. **Recognition:** the narrator sees their own error, limit, desire, or loss.
5. **Aftertone:** the essay withdraws from direct explanation and lets an image remain.

Do not keep emotion at full volume. Strong emotion is most convincing when surrounded by restraint.

### Language

Use varied sentence rhythm:

- Short sentences for perception, embarrassment, refusal, and turns.
- Medium sentences for scene movement.
- Longer sentences for memory, thought, and association.

Keep the prose mostly concrete. Use abstract nouns sparingly and make them arrive after objects or actions. A useful ratio is roughly:

- 60 percent scene and action.
- 25 percent reflection.
- 15 percent lyrical compression.

Use metaphor only when it clarifies an emotional relation. Avoid consecutive decorative metaphors.

### Ending

Good endings:

- Return to the opening object with altered meaning.
- Let a person continue an ordinary action after the narrator understands more.
- Leave a sentence unsent, a train missed, a room cleaned, a light still on.
- Offer a small factual image rather than a grand principle.

Bad endings:

- "So I understood life..."
- "We must cherish..."
- "Youth is..."
- A motivational slogan.
- A summary that explains every symbol.

## Style Intensities

- **Light:** use the framework with mostly plain prose; suitable for very personal or factual material.
- **Standard:** balanced scenes, reflection, and image threads; use for most long pieces.
- **Dense:** more associative movement and image recurrence; use only when the user asks for stronger literary texture or provides enough material.

Default to Standard. Dense style must still remain intelligible.

## Anti-Patterns

- Empty profundity: abstract ideas appearing before any lived evidence.
- Imitation accent: forced melancholy, overuse of rain, dusk, youth, stars, ruins, fate, silence, and eternity.
- Essay-contest ending: a final paragraph that converts the story into a lesson.
- Beautiful sentence pileup: lines that sound polished but do not advance scene, relation, or recognition.
- False experience: invented hardship or intimacy presented as factual user memory.
- Monochrome sadness: every paragraph has the same emotional color.
- Over-clever structure: fragmented form that hides the lack of emotional movement.
- Author shadowing: recognizable imitation of a specific writer, winner, or published work.

## Final Taste Test

Before delivering, ask privately:

- Can the reader retell what happened?
- Is there at least one object or gesture that changes meaning?
- Does reflection emerge from evidence?
- Is the ending quieter than the strongest emotion?
- Could the piece belong to this user's material rather than to a generic literary persona?
